Below is a list of the major changes to the five (5) existing CSF Functions from version 1.1: Identify (ID): Much of Identify (ID) has been moved to the new Govern (GV) function:&nbsp; ID.BE (now GV.OC),&nbsp; ID.GV, ID.RM (now GV.RM), and&nbsp; ID.SC (now GV.SC). Identify (ID) has a new Improvement (ID.IM) Category.&nbsp; Protect (PR): PR.AC has been moved to PR.AA, revised, and re-sequenced. PR.IP has been decomposed and its Subcategories moved to Govern, Identify, and Protect. PR.MA has been predominantly moved to the new ID.AM-08 Subcategory. PR.PT has been decomposed and its Subcategories moved to other PR Categories. There are two new PR Categories:&nbsp; PR.PS (Platform Security) and&nbsp; PR.IR (Technology Infrastructure Resilience). Detect (DE): DE.DP has been decomposed and its Subcategories moved to Govern, Identify, and Detect. Respond (RS): RS.RP has been moved to the new RS.MA, which covers the broader topic of incident management. RS.IM has been moved to ID.IM.&nbsp; Recover (RC): RC.RP has been significantly expanded. RC.IM has been moved to ID.IM. Do you have any resources to show the difference between CSF 1.1 to CSF 2.0? The crosswalk between CSF 1.1 and CSF 2.0 is an Informative Reference. It can be consumed in multiple ways: Directly to the Informative reference with the core - https://csrc.nist.gov/extensions/nudp/services/json/csf/download?olirids=all The Crosswalk from the OLIR catalog with more detail - https://csrc.nist.gov/csrc/media/Projects/olir/documents/submissions/CSFv1.1_to_CSFv2.0_CROSSWALK_20240220.xlsx Explanation for the transitions of individual Categories and Subcategories from CSF 1.1 to CSF 2.0, which are intended to aid anyone converting a CSF 1.1 Profile, mapping, or other CSF 1.1 Core-structured content to use the CSF 2.0 Core structure - CSF Supplemental Spreadsheet &nbsp; From the CSF 2.0 Tool page - CSF 2.0 Reference Tool Why are there gaps in the numbering of CSF 2.0 Subcategory identifiers? The numbering of the Subcategories is intentionally not sequential. Gaps in numbering indicate CSF v1.1 Subcategories that were relocated as part of the major update to CSF 2.0. Any Subcategory present in both CSF v1.1 and CSF 2.0 has a similar meaning in both. There was no removal of content from 1.1 to 2.0; the meaning of all 1.1 content is included within CSF 2.0. During the CSF 2.0 development process, many Subcategories were added to or changed from CSF 1.1. For example, PR.DS-3 from CSF v1.1 was moved to ID.AM-08 in CSF 2.0. As such, the PR.DS-3 Subcategory identifier was not reused in CSF 2.0. The downloadable PDF &nbsp;contains a comprehensive depiction of all withdrawn CSF v1.1 identifiers and where in CSF 2.0 the meaning associated with each of those identifiers has been incorporated. Why was Govern added as a new Function to CSF 2.0? The Govern Function was introduced to emphasize the critical role of cybersecurity governance in managing and reducing risk. Previously, governance activities were embedded within the Identify Function, limiting visibility into how governance supports all aspects of a cybersecurity program. By elevating governance activities, such as setting risk tolerances, defining roles and responsibilities, and establishing policies, the new Govern Function promotes clearer alignment between cybersecurity efforts, enterprise risk management, and legal obligations. This update also brings CSF 2.0 in line with other NIST frameworks, like the AI and Privacy Frameworks, which include similar governance-focused functions. Framework Basics What is the Cybersecurity Framework (CSF)? The CSF helps manage and reduce cybersecurity risks with a taxonomy of high-level outcomes that any organization can use to understand, assess, prioritize, and communicate its cybersecurity efforts. It also links to resources that provide additional guidance on practices and controls for achieving security outcomes. Among other things, the CSF fosters risk and cybersecurity management communications between and among internal and external stakeholders. Where do I get started? The NIST Framework website has many resources to help organizations implement the Framework. The Framework&nbsp; Quick Start Guide Repository provides direction and guidance to those seeking to improve cybersecurity risk management via utilization of the NIST Cybersecurity Framework. The Resource Repository includes approaches, methodologies, implementation guides, mappings to the Framework, case studies, educational materials, Internet resource centers (e.g., blogs, document stores), example profiles, and other Framework document templates. Is my organization required to use the CSF? NIST is not a regulatory agency, and most organizations use the CSF on a voluntary basis. However, Executive Order 13800, Strengthening the Cybersecurity of Federal Networks and Critical Infrastructure , made the Framework mandatory for U.S. Federal Government agencies, and some companies require the CSF for their customers or within their supply chain. Does the CSF provide a recommended checklist of what all organizations should do? No. The CSF provides a series of outcomes to prioritize and address cybersecurity risks but does not specify actions for meeting those outcomes.&nbsp; Who helped to develop the Framework? For the past 10 years, NIST has collaborated with stakeholders in industry, academia, government, and international forums to develop the CSF. Thousands of detailed comments and suggestions from users around the world have helped shape the CSF you see today.&nbsp; NIST routinely engages users through three primary activities:&nbsp; Community outreach and participation in meetings, events, and roundtable dialogues Solicitation of direct feedback through requests for information (RFIs), requests for comments (RFCs), and the CSF team’s email address at&nbsp; cyberframework [at] nist.gov ( cyberframework[at]nist[dot]gov ) &nbsp; Research into relevant resources and references published by industry, academia, and government More information on the development of the CSF can be found in the Development Archive . How can we obtain NIST certification for our CSF products and implementations? NIST does not offer certifications or endorsements of CSF-related products, implementations, or services, and there are no plans to develop a conformity assessment program.&nbsp; However, NIST does share resources that demonstrate the CSF’s real-world applications and benefits. By sharing your organization’s experiences and successes, you can inspire new use cases and help others understand the CSF. To contribute to these resources, contact cyberframework [at] nist.gov ( cyberframework[at]nist[dot]gov ) . Do I need reprint permission to use material from a NIST publication? No. Many organizations in the private and public sectors (including federal agencies) use the CSF as a tool to manage cybersecurity risks.&nbsp; Will organizations with mature cybersecurity programs benefit from using the CSF, or is it just for “beginners”? Any organization — whether its cybersecurity program is new or mature — can use the CSF to manage cybersecurity risks. How you use the CSF will vary depending on your current state and priorities.&nbsp; How is the CSF being used today? Organizations are using the CSF in a variety of ways, including:&nbsp; Aligning organizational policies with industry best practices, standards, and regulations Assessing cybersecurity risks Improving communication with stakeholders and executive leadership Sharing cybersecurity expectations with business partners and suppliers Raising awareness The NIST CSF website also provides specific examples of how organizations have used the Framework.&nbsp; Framework Components What is the Framework Core? The Framework Core is a set of cybersecurity activities, desired outcomes, and applicable references. It consists of six concurrent and continuous Functions:&nbsp; Govern Identify Protect Detect Respond Recover The Framework Core then identifies underlying key Categories and Subcategories for each Function. How your organization addresses the Category and Subcategory outcome statements in each Function will provide a high-level, strategic, and life-cycle view of your cybersecurity risk management program. You can then share those outcomes and expectations with executives, implementation and operations personnel, partners, suppliers, and customers. Why does NIST use outcomes within the Framework Cores rather than specify a process? NIST has designed the CSF to be as flexible as possible. Outcomes are non-prescriptive and can be accomplished through any number of activities, whereas a specific process is inherently limiting. The outcomes you select from the Core’s Categories and Subcategories are the “what.” The CSF Informative References, Implementation Examples, Quick Start Guides, and other resources help your organization figure out the “how.”&nbsp; What are Organizational Framework Profiles? An Organizational CSF Profile describes an organization’s current and target cybersecurity posture. It will help you understand, tailor, assess, prioritize, and communicate Core outcomes based on your organization’s specific mission objectives, stakeholder expectations, threat landscape, requirements, and leading practices.&nbsp; There are two primary types of Organizational Profiles: A Current Profile describes the Core outcomes that your organization is currently achieving and how.&nbsp; A Target Profile describes the Core outcomes that your organization wants to achieve and considers anticipated changes to your cybersecurity posture, such as new requirements, new technology adoption, and threat intelligence trends. What are Community Framework Profiles? Community Framework Profiles offer cybersecurity risk management guidance for multiple organizations to address a sector, technology, or challenge. These profiles differ from Organizational Framework Profiles which are generally not shared publicly.&nbsp;Community profiles reflect a consensus point of view about cybersecurity risk management and may be used as the basis of, or to inform, Organizational Target Profiles. View the CSF 2.0 Profiles page for more information.&nbsp; What are Framework Implementation Tiers? Implementation Tiers inform Organizational Profiles by describing how cybersecurity risks will be managed with a range of outcomes:&nbsp; Partial (Tier 1) Risk Informed (Tier 2)&nbsp; Repeatable (Tier 3) Adaptive (Tier 4) The Tiers reflect a progression from informal, ad hoc responses to approaches that are agile, risk-informed, and continuously improving. What are Informative References? Informative References provide an easy way to link CSF outcomes statements to specific recommendations by showing the relationships between organizational concepts in Focal Documents and specific sections, sentences, or phrases in Reference Documents. The discrete concepts of the Focal Document are called Focal Document elements, and the specific sections, sentences, or phrases of the Reference Document are called Reference Document elements. Many Informative References are contained in the OLIR catalog . What are Implementation Examples? Implementation Examples are concise, action-oriented suggestions for achieving the outcomes of CSF Subcategories. They complement the guidance provided by Informative References. The examples are not a comprehensive list of all actions that could be taken by an organization to achieve an outcome, nor do they represent a baseline of required actions to address cybersecurity risk. View the CSF 2.0 Tool to view implementation examples.&nbsp; Why does NIST differentiate between the Framework’s Core, Implementation Examples, and Informative References? Why are Informative References and Implementation Examples not considered a part of the Core? The CSF Core is a set of cybersecurity outcomes composed of Functions, Categories, and Subcategories. The Implementation Examples help users understand possible ways to fulfill aspects of the Core. Informative References help users understand the relationship between the CSF and other common standards and frameworks, providing more context for each outcome. Neither Implementation Examples nor Informative References are meant to be the only ways to fulfill outcomes in the Core, and they are not an exhaustive or complete listing. The CSF 2.0 Informative References page is located at https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework/informative-references . Are there plans to update CSF 2.0 to address AI? There are not any immediate plans to update the CSF Core to address AI. However, there are many existing outcomes within the CSF that can help organizations address cybersecurity risks that can arise from the use of AI, for example: cybersecurity risks posed by third party AI tools are understood and monitored (GV.SC-07); inventories of software, including AI, are conducted (ID.AM-02); technology stacks that run AI need to be securely patched and configured (PR.PS-01,02); employees are provided with awareness and training so that they possess the knowledge and skills to perform general tasks, including working with AI tools, with cybersecurity risks in mind (PR.AT-01), etc.&nbsp; NIST also has other complementary efforts, including but not limited to: The&nbsp; NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) &nbsp;is intended for voluntary use and to improve the ability to incorporate trustworthiness considerations into the design, development, use, and evaluation of AI products, services, and systems. The Cyber AI Profile . This project focuses on how to use existing frameworks, such as the CSF, to assist organizations as they face new or expanded risks related to AI development and use.&nbsp; The Control Overlays for Securing AI Systems (COSAiS) project , which will develop a series of overlays for securing AI systems using the&nbsp;NIST Special Publication (SP) 800-53 controls.&nbsp; Using the Framework Does the CSF help weigh the cost-effectiveness of cybersecurity risk management? Yes. Your organization should use the CSF to identify activities that are most important to your mission, prioritize expenditures, and consider the impacts of the investment.&nbsp; Can the CSF help manage risks to assets that are not under my direct management? Yes. The Functions, Categories, and Subcategories of the Framework Core are applicable whether you are operating your own assets or another party is operating assets as a service for you. In fact, CSF 2.0 has been revised to emphasize supply chain considerations and can be used as evaluation criteria for selecting among multiple product and service providers. The CSF can also help you state your expected outcomes and activities with external suppliers, services providers, and system integrators. Should the CSF be applied to and by the entire organization or just to the IT department? The CSF is a flexible framework that can drive consistency and build awareness both within individual departments and across the entire organization. However, it is most effective when implemented as part of your organization’s broader enterprise risk management (ERM) program, rather than being limited to the IT department. The CSF’s Functions, Categories, and Subcategories apply to all levels of the organization, from executive leadership to individual operating units, and even supply chain partners. What is the role of senior executives and Board members in using the CSF? The CSF enables senior leaders to better understand, direct, and manage cybersecurity risks to the organization by increasing awareness, enabling prioritization, improving communications among leadership (e.g., CIO, CEO, Executive Board, etc.), and placing cybersecurity considerations in the context of broader enterprise risks.&nbsp; How can organizations measure the effectiveness of the CSF? The CSF’s effectiveness depends on your organization’s goals, so measuring that effectiveness is left to your discretion. While NIST does not recommend a specific model for measuring effectiveness, you may want to consider the following:&nbsp; Does your organization want an overall assessment of cybersecurity-related risks, policies, and processes? Do you have a specific outcome in mind, such as improving customer confidence or cybersecurity management with suppliers? Do I need a consultant to implement or assess the CSF? Can NIST recommend a consultant? No. While some organizations leverage the expertise of external experts and organizations, they are not required to implement the CSF. NIST does not provide recommendations for consultants or assessors. Does the CSF require any specific technologies or products? No. The CSF is designed to be flexible and work with the products and services you choose to acquire and use. It encourages technological innovation and keeping up with the dynamic cybersecurity landscape by aiming for strong cybersecurity protection without being tied to specific offerings or current technology. How is cyber resilience reflected in the CSF? Cyber resiliency is the ability to anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to adverse conditions, stresses, attacks, or compromises on systems that use or are enabled by cyber resources, regardless of the source ( NIST SP 800-160, Volume 2, Rev. 1 ). Cyber resiliency has a strong relationship to cybersecurity but — like privacy — represents a distinct problem domain and solution space. Cyber resiliency supports mission assurance for missions that depend on IT and OT systems in a contested environment. When Implementing the CSF an organization can review their current state profile to help determine if cyber resiliency is adequately supported, whether additional elements are necessary, and how to close any gaps. Target states will likely require a combination of fault-tolerance, adversity-tolerance, and graceful degradation in relation to mission goals, and the CSF supports these high-level organizational discussions.&nbsp; What is the CSF’s role in supporting an organization’s compliance requirements? The CSF provides a flexible, risk-based approach to help your organization manage cybersecurity risks and achieve its cybersecurity objectives. Those objectives may be informed by and derived from organizational cybersecurity requirements and applicable laws, rules, and regulations.&nbsp; The Framework’s Govern Function includes a subcategory (GV.OC-03) with a specific outcome statement relevant to compliance: “ Legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements regarding cybersecurity — including privacy and civil liberties obligations — are understood and managed.” How do I use the CSF to prioritize cybersecurity activities? The CSF can help your organization align and prioritize its cybersecurity activities with its business and mission requirements, risk tolerances, and resources. For example, Current Profiles indicate the cybersecurity outcomes that are currently being achieved, while Target Profiles indicate the outcomes needed to achieve the desired cybersecurity risk management goals. Comparing these Profiles may reveal gaps to be addressed to meet cybersecurity risk management objectives. An action plan to address these gaps and fulfill a given Category or Subcategory of the Framework Core can help you set priorities that consider your organization’s business needs and risk management processes. Additionally, the Framework Tiers can help your organization determine the extent to which cybersecurity risk management is informed by business needs and is integrated into overall risk management practices. Understanding cybersecurity risk tolerance will help your organization prioritize cybersecurity activities and make informed decisions about cybersecurity expenditures.&nbsp; Your organization may choose to handle risk in different ways (e.g., mitigating the risk, transferring the risk, avoiding the risk, or accepting the risk), depending on the potential impacts to the delivery of critical services.&nbsp; Small Business Use Does the CSF apply to small businesses? Yes. The CSF was developed for organizations of any size, sector, or maturity. To assist smaller organizations, the &nbsp; CSF 2.0 Small Business Quick Start Guide helps small-to-medium sized businesses (SMB) kick-start their cybersecurity risk management strategy, specifically those who have modest or no cybersecurity plans in place. As more CSF 2.0 small business resources are created, they will be archived on the Cybersecurity Framework page of the NIST Small Business Cybersecurity Corner website. U.S. Federal Agency Use Does the Cybersecurity Framework support NIST’s Information Communication Technology Risk Management approach (NIST SP 800-221)? Yes, since cybersecurity is a key element of information communication technology (ICT), implementation and improvement of an enterprise cybersecurity program through the CSF directly benefits overall ICT Risk Management (ICTRM).&nbsp; The CSF provides a high-level method to determine enterprise objectives, identify and protect key resources, and collaborate on plans to detect, respond to, and recover from cyber incidents. ICTRM, as an integral part of Enterprise Risk Management (ERM), provides structured templates for prioritizing assets based on the business impacts of positive and negative risk, record and refine risk expectations, and register risk scenarios and treatment at all enterprise levels.&nbsp; Through the holistic and common language of the CSF, paired with repeatable communications artifacts as described in SP 800-221, ICTRM in support of ERM is consistently and effectively maintained. Are U.S. federal agencies required to apply the CSF to federal information systems? Yes. The 2017 Executive Order on Strengthening the Cybersecurity of Federal Networks and Critical Infrastructure states: Each agency head shall provide a risk management report to the Secretary of Homeland Security and the Director of the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) within 90 days of the date of this order…and describe the agency’s action plan to implement the Framework. NIST developed Interagency Report (IR) 8170, Approaches for Federal Agencies to Use the Cybersecurity Framework , to provide federal agencies with guidance on how the CSF can complement existing risk management practices and improve cybersecurity risk management programs. What is the relationship between the Framework and NIST’s Managing Information Security Risk: Organization, Mission, and Information System View (Special Publication 800-39)? NIST SP 800-39 is a complimentary resource to help CSF users better manage cybersecurity risks, providing guidance on a process to Frame, Assess, Respond, and Monitor risk and how to apply it across all levels of an organization.&nbsp; Organizations can use the guidance from SP 800-39 to better understand key concepts introduced in the CSF, such as: governance, developing a risk management strategy, determining risk tolerance, conducting risk assessment, identifying and selecting appropriate risk responses, and monitoring risk at the organization, mission/business, and system levels. Note that SP 800-37 includes additional guidance primarily focused at the system level. &nbsp; What is the relationship between the Framework and NIST’s Guide for Applying the Risk Management Framework to Federal Information Systems (SP 800-37)? The Framework provides a common language and taxonomy to better manage cybersecurity risk at an enterprise (organization) level.&nbsp; NIST SP 800-37 applies the (Frame, Assess, Respond and Monitor) concepts from SP 800-39 in a repeatable, flexible process to protect systems from information security and privacy risk.&nbsp; The process includes steps and tasks to prepare for risk management at all levels, but guidance is primarily focused at the system level. SP 800-37 provides a methodology to select CSF outcomes, and includes implementation guidance for how to prepare for risk management, categorize information and systems, select, implement and assess the appropriate [SP 800-53] controls to manage risk, authorize the system to operate, and continuously monitor the controls and system.&nbsp; Mappings between the CSF Functions, Categories and Subcategories to the respective Steps and Tasks of SP 800-37 can be found at&nbsp; https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/olir/informative-reference-catalog#/ . Relationship Between the Framework and Other Approaches and Initiatives What is the relationship between the CSF and the Workforce Framework for Cybersecurity (NICE Framework)? While the CSF and the Workforce Framework for Cybersecurity (NICE Framework) were developed separately, each complements the other by describing a hierarchical approach to achieving cybersecurity goals. In the CSF, each of the Core Subcategory outcomes is accomplished as a task (or set of tasks) by someone in one or more work roles; the NICE Framework ( NIST Special Publication 800-181r1 ) similarly describes Work Roles containing detailed sets of Task, Knowledge, and Skill (TKS) statements. Thus, both the CSF and the NICE Framework deal with activities in the form of tasks: the CSF focuses on “what” those tasks are intended to accomplish in terms of cybersecurity risk management, while the NICE Framework explains “by whom” different tasks are carried out, in cybersecurity risk management as well as other cybersecurity-related functions. The NICE Framework was developed and is maintained by NICE , which is a partnership among government, academia, and the private sector with a mission to energize and promote a robust network and ecosystem of cybersecurity education, training, and workforce development.&nbsp; What is the relationship between the CSF and the NIST Privacy Framework? The Privacy Framework follows the same risk- and outcome-based structure of the CSF and is composed of three parts: the Core, Profiles, and Implementation Tiers. The Frameworks are aligned to reduce complexity and address the full scope of privacy risks that arise from how organizations collect, store, use, and share information and how individuals interact with products and services.&nbsp; Details about how the CSF and Privacy Framework functions align and intersect can be found in the Privacy Framework FAQs .&nbsp; What is the relationship between the CSF and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) Each framework provides a structured approach to managing risks, but they focus on different domains. Specifically, the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) offers guidelines to manage from cybersecurity risks and it is broadly applicable across all types of systems and organizations. In contrast, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) specifically addresses the unique risks associated with AI systems throughout their lifecycle. This includes guidance for those who design, develop, deploy, use, and are affected by AI, aiming to promote trustworthy and responsible AI. While both frameworks help organizations manage risk, they differ in focus. AI risks shouldn’t be managed in isolation; integrating them with cybersecurity and other risks leads to more effective, streamlined risk management. NIST’s cybersecurity resources complement its AI efforts, supporting a unified approach that helps industry, government, and the public address evolving risks across both domains. Is the CSF aligned with international cybersecurity initiatives and standards? Yes. Global alignment is integral to avoid confusion, the duplication of effort, and conflicting expectations in global business environments. NIST is actively engaged with international standards-developing organizations to promote consistent approaches, which have in turn been adopted by other countries and international entities. The CSF has also been translated to numerous other languages.&nbsp; What is the relationship between the Internet of Things (IoT) and the CSF? Do we need frameworks specific to IoT, supply chain management, or cloud services? CSF 2.0 was designed to be applicable to a broad range of technologies, including the Internet of Things (IoT), supply chain management, and cloud services. NIST will continually evaluate the CSF and ensure that it evolves with emerging technologies to retain this critical alignment. NIST also welcomes observations from all parties regarding the CSF’s relevance to IoT and will vet those observations with the NIST Cybersecurity for IoT Program . What is the relationship between the CSF and the Baldrige Cybersecurity Excellence Builder? The Baldrige Cybersecurity Excellence Builder is an assessment tool that gives organizations a way to measure the effectiveness of their cybersecurity risk management efforts. It combines the systems perspective and business practices of the Baldridge Excellence Framework with the concepts, principles, and objectives of the CSF to identify opportunities to improve overall organizational performance.&nbsp; What is the relationship between the CSF and threat frameworks? Both types of frameworks are critical tools for making risk decisions and evaluating safeguards. Threat frameworks characterize malicious cyber activity in varying degrees of detail to help you understand and prioritize current and potential attacks against a given system, infrastructure, service, or organization. The CSF’s controls provide safeguards against many of those activities, including the risk of adversarial attack. As circumstances change and evolve, threat frameworks provide the basis for reevaluating and refining risk decisions using collected data, a common lexicon, and the CSF.&nbsp; Example threat frameworks include the U.S. Office of the Director of National Intelligence (ODNI) Cyber Threat Framework (CTF), Lockheed Martin’s Cyber Kill Chain® , and the MITRE Adversarial Tactics, Techniques &amp; Common Knowledge (ATT&amp;CK) model. Each threat framework depicts a progression of attack steps in which successive steps build on the last step. At the highest level of the model, the ODNI CTF relays this information using four Stages: Preparation, Engagement, Presence, and Consequence. These Stages are decomposed into a hierarchy of Objectives, Actions, and Indicators at three increasingly detailed levels of the CTF. The structure is similar to the Functions, Categories, and Subcategories of the CSF and empower professionals of varying disciplines to participate in identifying, assessing, and managing security controls. NIST recommends that organizations use a combination of cyber threat frameworks (e.g., ODNI Cyber Threat Framework) and cybersecurity frameworks (e.g., the CSF) to make risk decisions. What is the relationship between the CSF and the Cybersecurity and Privacy Reference Tool (CPRT)? The CPRT provides a centralized, standardized, and modernized mechanism for managing reference datasets (and offers a consistent format for accessing reference data from various NIST cybersecurity and privacy standards, guidelines, and Frameworks). This data can be exported into different data formats so users can draw upon multiple NIST resources to build their own cybersecurity and privacy guidance. What is the relationship between the CSF and the National Online Informative References (OLIR) Program? Informative References for the CSF are populated by mapped content from the National Online Informative References (OLIR) Program, which is a NIST effort to facilitate subject-matter experts (SMEs) in defining standardized Online Informative References (OLIRs) between elements of their cybersecurity, privacy, and workforce documents and elements of other cybersecurity, privacy, and workforce documents, like the CSF.&nbsp; This stage of the OLIR Program’s evolution has primarily focused on relationships to cybersecurity and privacy documents. By following this approach, cybersecurity practitioners can use the OLIR Program as a mechanism for communicating with owners and users of other cybersecurity documents. OLIRs use a simple standard format and are searchable in a centralized repository at https://csrc.nist.gov/projects/olir/informative-reference-catalog . NIST Interagency or Internal Report (NIST IR) 8278, Rev. 1 and IR 8278A, Rev. 1 provide more details the OLIR program. IR 8278, Rev. 1 focuses on the OLIR program overview and uses, while IR 8278A, Rev. 1 &nbsp;provides submission guidance for OLIR developers. NIST is happy to aid in this process and can be contacted at olir [at] nist.gov (olir[at]nist[dot]gov) . What is the difference between the CSF and cybersecurity controls released by NIST? The NIST cybersecurity control sets, such as SP 800-53 or SP 800-171, serve distinct but complementary purposes from the Cybersecurity Framework. The CSF Functions, Categories, and Subcategories serve as an organizing principle, helping organizations define their cybersecurity objectives, understand the importance of those objectives, and organize the various components of their program in a cohesive manner. In support, cybersecurity controls provide safeguards or countermeasures to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of a system and its information. For organizations developing or evolving their cybersecurity programs, beginning with a framework like the CSF ensures a clear, risk-informed foundation to assist in selecting and implementing security controls to meet the organizations cyber risk goals. NIST engages with international&nbsp;government and industry stakeholders throughout the world on the CSF and other NIST cybersecurity and privacy resources. &nbsp;&nbsp;NIST works closely with&nbsp;the State Department, the International Trade Administration, and other U.S. government agencies as well as with industry partners to identify and engage in opportunities internationally to share information on the CSF. &nbsp;As a result of this direct engagement and participation&nbsp;in various multinational forums, the CSF is used widely internationally; versions 1.1 and 1.0 have been translated into 13 languages, and the CSF 2.0 has been translated into 11 languages to-date. NIST expects that the CSF will continue to be translated throughout the world. &nbsp;These translations as well as other international adaptations of the CSF can be found on the NIST International Cybersecurity and Privacy Resources website . &nbsp;NOTE:&nbsp;&nbsp;The CSF and NIST publications&nbsp;are generally in the public domain and not subject to copyright in the U.S. &nbsp;If you have developed a translation of the CSF, please reach out to cyberframework [at] nist.gov ( cyberframework[at]nist[dot]gov ) &nbsp;and someone will contact you regarding the process for granting permission to use the CSF outside of the U.S.&nbsp; NIST also engages with Standards Developing Organizations (SDOs) on efforts related to cybersecurity and privacy, such as the CSF to promote a standards-based approach to these areas that scale&nbsp;internationally. &nbsp;For over a decade, NIST’s work with&nbsp;the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), in conjunction with the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) has helped to align multiple cybersecurity documents. ISO/IEC resources now allow organizations to build cybersecurity frameworks and organize controls&nbsp;using the CSF Functions. NIST plans to continue working with ISO/IEC to continue this international alignment. NIST encourages&nbsp;international&nbsp;participation on the CSF and welcomes conversation with&nbsp;international stakeholders. To submit ideas or questions or to discuss opportunities to engage, contact us at cyberframework [at] nist.gov ( cyberframework[at]nist[dot]gov ) .&nbsp; What is the difference between a translation and adaptation of the CSF? A translation is considered a direct, literal translation of the language of a version of the CSF. No content or language is altered in a translation. Current translations of the CSF 2.0 can be found on the Translations page. In contrast, an adaptation is considered a version of the CSF that substantially references language and content but incorporates new, original content. An adaptation can be in any language. Current adaptations can be found on the International Resources page. Does NIST encourage translations of the CSF? If so, is there a procedure to follow? Yes. NIST encourages translations of CSF 2.0. After an independent check, NIST will typically post links to translations on external websites. These links appear on NIST’s Translations of NIST Cybersecurity and Privacy Resources page. The sign-up box is located at the bottom-right hand side on each CSF web page or on the left-hand side of other NIST pages.
